Who Gets to Define Respect?

Three weeks ago, LeBron James moved during the national anthem at Madison Square Garden. He didn't kneel. Some people saw a stretch. Some saw a golf swing. Two days later, elected officials called it a "total disgrace" and demanded an apology to veterans.

You've probably heard somebody's take by now. You may have your own. Wherever you land, here's a way to have that conversation and still like each other afterward.

Clarify: "When you say he disrespected the country, what did you see that bothered you?"

Probe: "What does the anthem mean to you? Who do you think about when it plays?"

Then listen, because the answer might be a grandfather, or a friend who didn't come home. That's real, and it deserves your full attention.

Probe: "Can you understand how someone else might hear it differently? Are you aware of the history of the song, and the verse most of us never sing?"

Or, more directly: "If an anthem talked about killing your ancestors, would you stand for it?"

Every one of those questions leads to the same place. You're asking them to consider why someone might not feel respected by this song, and might not want to show it that respect.

Redirect: "Would you be open to learning more about why some people are offended by the song?"

If they say yes, here are two minutes to start with: The Third Verse. Jeffery Robinson walks through Francis Scott Key and the verse we don't sing at ballgames.

You don't have to settle it in one conversation. If you both leave understanding each other a little better, that's a good night.

APRIL

They Hoped for 50

On April 11, we walked into the Wesley United Methodist Church hall in Concord for an event hosted by Unitarian Universalist Action New Hampshire. People there had been at it for a while. The leadership began working on the logistics back in October of 2025.

Here's how they told it to their own community nine days later:

“We hoped for 50 people, but 69 people (standing room only) assembled in a church hall to hear and meet Kyle Williams and Kamal Carter and start the journey to having one anti-racist at every table in New Hampshire.”

One anti-racist at every table. That's their line, and we wish we'd written it.

Their newsletter also shared what people said afterward. One person had "started using it at school." Somebody summed up the whole day in three words: "A slam dunk." And one person, writing about the 80-minute video we start everybody with, said this: "I was shocked at how much I had not known and had not been taught."

Most of you reading this have already seen The History of Race in America. What you may not know is that we've cut it into 13 short moments, two or three minutes each, so you can send one directly to the person who needs it. Browse the clips.

Before we left, people were asking us to come back for their schools, synagogues, churches and organizations. We promised to help build a network in New Hampshire. They ended up doing most of the building, and that group never stopped meeting. We'll tell you that story in December.

APRIL

Civil Rights Pilgrimage

A historic Civil Rights Pilgrimage took place April 16 through 18. We led a group of 43 predominantly white pastors and church leaders from the New England Conference of the United Methodist Church, including Bishop Thomas Bickerton. Some members of the New York Conference attended as well.

It started long before the trip. Over the past year, several hundred pastors and leaders in the New England Conference participated in A Long Talk, after we spoke to more than 600 church leaders at the NEUMC Annual Conference in the summer of 2025. This pilgrimage was a culminating activity.

They flew from Boston, landed in Atlanta, got on the bus and went straight to Ebenezer Baptist Church. We fellowshipped, we strategized, and we mobilized. Next, we walked across the street to the King Center and had a private presentation on Kingian nonviolence by leaders from the King Center. After that, we got back on the bus and headed to Alabama.

The next day we were in Selma. It's really hard to put this whole experience into words. The history, the inspiration, the emotion, the Edmund Pettus Bridge.

Press play on the video below. You'll see our last day was spent in Montgomery at the Legacy Sites and the National Memorial for Peace and Justice. What Bryan Stevenson has built in Montgomery is unlike anything in the world. It's a must-see in your lifetime.

MAY

Still Walking on Air

We started with the University of Oregon's DEPARTMENT OF PHYSICAL EDUCATION AND RECREATION staff on Zoom in April, then went to Eugene in May to sit with them in person.

The impact on the day of the in-person session is important. Equally as important is sustained action after the session. What we really want to know is what happens the next week, and the week after that, and the week after that. One of the leaders in the department told us people came in that Monday "still kind of walking on air."

If you work on a campus, you know how rare a Monday like that is.

JUNE

Creators, Not Conformists

Last week we told you about the session with MLB's own Washington Nationals in February. Here's what came next.

In March, Kyle toured the Nationals Youth Baseball Academy. On June 23, we came back for about 28 of its summer staff, ages 19 to 40, program graduates alongside teachers and coaches. They were getting ready for camp, and kids would be looking to them all summer.

One of the leaders in the Academy gave us the phrase for the day: "creators, not conformists." It went right on our title slide.

We spent the morning on why this matters and how CPR works. At lunch, we asked what they might actually hear from kids this summer, and they practiced on their phones with the CPR Mini Coach. In the afternoon they got in groups of three. Two people practiced, one watched, and then they rotated.

That coach is free, and it'll play the other side for you too.
